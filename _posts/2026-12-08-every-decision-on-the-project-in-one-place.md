*Part 4 of the Work Graph series. The costliest sentence on a project is "I thought we agreed otherwise." A register of decisions is the cheapest way to avoid it.*

Projects do not fail because decisions were not made. They stumble because decisions were made somewhere nobody can find. The client approved a change in a call. The partner ruled on a staffing question in a chat thread. A scope boundary was settled in a hallway and repeated, slightly differently, in two follow-up emails. When a disagreement arrives, the team has to prove what happened with whatever evidence survives.

## How decisions get lost

### 1. The Meeting-Shaped Hole

A decision recorded as one line in a long transcript is technically on file and practically gone. Nobody can search for a thing they do not know was said.

### 2. The Chat That Scrolled Away

Decisions made in a conversation sit between jokes and status updates. The thread that settled a question last month is forty screens above where anyone is looking.

### 3. The Decision Without a Date or a Name

"We agreed to drop that requirement" is useless in a dispute without when, who agreed, and who was present.

## One register per project

With the Work Graph, every decision on a project is a **record**: the statement, the date, who decided, and a link to where it came from, whether that is a stretch of a meeting transcript or a message in a project conversation. The **decision register** is the project's list of them, found in the **Work** tab alongside meetings, tasks and the timeline.

Decisions get there from two directions. People record them directly, from a meeting page or a conversation. And Parle can [suggest them from a transcript](/blog/2026/12/03/decisions-and-actions-suggested-never-assumed/), which a person confirms. Confirmed and suggested decisions are shown differently, so the register never presents a guess as an agreement.

This is the point of connecting the pieces. A decision in the register is not an isolated line; it is linked to the meeting where it was made, the people who attended, and the project it governs.

## What it answers

**What did we decide about X?** The register is the first place to look, and it is the first place the assistant looks. Ask the project's assistant "what did we decide about the rollout date" and it answers from the decision records and cites them, rather than paraphrasing a search across everything.

**Who decided that?** Each record names the decider, matched to the people who were actually in the meeting or on the project. Where the decider was an external attendee who is not a Parle user, that is recorded as well.

**Can I see the original?** Every record links back to its source. Opening it takes you to the passage, read with your own access, so the register never reveals more than you were already allowed to see.

## What it deliberately leaves out

The register is a list of records. It does not count decisions per person, rank contributors or show who "decides the most." People appear as names on decisions, never as numbers, which is covered in [Built so it cannot score your people](/blog/2027/01/07/built-so-it-cannot-score-your-people/).

Records can be corrected. If a decision was recorded wrongly, a person with the right to do so edits or removes it, and the change is part of the project's history.

## Why we built it

A firm's memory of what it agreed with a client is an asset, and most firms keep it in the most perishable form available. The register turns it into something a new joiner, a returning partner or an assistant can read in a minute. It also gives the team something to point to when the question is not "what happened" but "what did we say we would do."
