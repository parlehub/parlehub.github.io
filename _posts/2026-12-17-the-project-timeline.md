*Part 7 of the Work Graph series. Every project has a story. The timeline is what lets someone read it instead of being told it.*

A new consultant joins an engagement in month four. They are capable, and they are missing four months of context. Someone, usually the busiest person on the team, gives them a briefing. The briefing is accurate and partial; it reflects what that person remembers and thinks is important. The newcomer then spends their first fortnight finding out what was left out.

Onboarding by conversation does not scale, and it is not repeatable. The knowledge exists. It is scattered across meetings, decisions, files and membership changes that no one has ever laid out in order.

## Why history is hard to read

### 1. The Briefing Bias

A verbal summary is shaped by who gives it. The newcomer receives one version of events and does not know it is one version.

### 2. The Order Problem

Meetings are in a calendar, decisions are in notes, documents are in a library, and who joined when is in an admin screen. Each has its own order. Nothing puts them in one.

### 3. The Missing Reason

A person left the project in September, and a decision was made two weeks earlier. Seen together, the two events explain each other. Seen apart, they explain nothing.

## One history for the project

The **Timeline** view in the project's **Work** tab is a single, scrollable history. It brings together:

- **Meetings**, in the order they happened.
- **Decisions**, shown beside the meeting where they were made.
- **Documents**, as they were added or changed.
- **People joining or leaving** the project.

Because these are connected in the Work Graph and not merely sorted by date, each entry links to the others. A decision links to its meeting, the meeting to its attendees, and a document to the project it belongs to. Scrolling back through a month is reading what happened in the order it happened, with the reasons beside the results.

The milestones and dates accepted from the [statement of work](/blog/2026/11/26/from-sow-to-project-plan/) anchor the same history, so the plan and what actually happened sit on one line.

## The default view of a project

For projects using the Work Graph, the timeline is one part of the **project dashboard**, which is the first thing a member sees on opening the project. It shows the timeline, the deliverables, the meetings, the active conversations, new files and the budget burn together. A member returning after two weeks away sees where the project stands before asking anyone.

## Who sees what

The timeline shows each person only the entries they are allowed to see. A meeting restricted to part of the team does not appear for a member outside it, and the timeline does not leave a gap that reveals it. The rules are covered in [Who can see what](/blog/2026/12/29/who-can-see-what/).

It also shows no figures about individuals. People appear as the names on events, never as a count of how many meetings someone attended or how active they have been.

## Why we built it

A project's history is an asset that most firms treat as a by-product. The timeline makes it a product. It shortens onboarding because the newcomer can read what a briefing would have summarized, and it shortens handovers, because the person leaving does not have to remember to write it all down.
